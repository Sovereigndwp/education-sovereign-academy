# Worked example 1 — Algebra 2 take-home task (the wedge cell)

**Status:** draft for Dalia's approval · 2026-09-04. The assignment below is written to be a *typical* Algebra 2 take-home modeling task — the kind that sits in a shared drive in most departments. It is not a real teacher's submission; Dalia should replace it with one of her own in Phase 0 and compare her audit to this one.

Part A is the assignment exactly as a teacher would hand it out. Part B is the full audit in the template form. Part C is our notes to Dalia (not sent to the teacher).

---

## Part A — The original assignment, as written by the teacher

> **Algebra 2 — Unit 4 Take-Home Task**
> **Modeling with Quadratic Functions**
> Due: one week from today · 20 points · Individual work · Graphing calculator or Desmos allowed
>
> Choose **one** of the following situations:
>
> **A. Projectile.** A water balloon is launched upward from the top of a 12-foot platform with an initial upward velocity of 40 feet per second. (Use *h(t) = −16t² + v₀t + h₀*.)
>
> **B. Revenue.** The junior class sells spirit shirts. At $10 each they sell 200 shirts. For every $1 they raise the price, they sell 10 fewer shirts.
>
> **C. Area.** You have 60 metres of fencing to build a rectangular pen against the side of a barn. The barn wall forms one side, so fencing is needed on only three sides.
>
> **Part 1 (4 pts).** Define your variables and write a quadratic function that models your situation. Explain where each number in your equation comes from.
>
> **Part 2 (4 pts).** Rewrite your function in a different form (standard, vertex, or factored). State the vertex and the zeros. Show your work.
>
> **Part 3 (4 pts).** Graph your function on the axes provided, or attach a labelled Desmos screenshot. Label the vertex, the intercepts, and a reasonable domain.
>
> **Part 4 (5 pts).** Interpret in context. What does the vertex tell you about your situation? What do the zeros mean? What is a reasonable domain, and why?
>
> **Part 5 (3 pts).** Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences) explaining how quadratic functions help us solve problems like this one, and describe one limitation of your model.
>
> **Rubric**
>
> | Part | 4 / full | 2–3 | 0–1 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1. Model | Correct function; every coefficient explained | Correct function; explanation incomplete | Function incorrect or unexplained |
> | 2. Forms | Correct conversion, vertex, zeros; work shown | Minor algebra error | Major errors / no work |
> | 3. Graph | Accurate, fully labelled, sensible domain | Missing labels or domain | Inaccurate |
> | 4. Interpretation (5) | Vertex, zeros, and domain all explained correctly in context | Two of three | One or none |
> | 5. Paragraph (3) | Clear explanation and a specific limitation | Vague limitation | Missing |

### 2 · Where a current model can substitute for the evidence

```

Judgment: A current model can produce the whole artifact — because every target is
```

### 3 · What thinking must remain visible

If the redesign preserves nothing else, it must keep this visible:

```
The invariant: the student's own attribution of each coefficient to a feature of the situation
(T1), and the student's own reading of the vertex and zeros as claims about that situation
(T3, T4). Those two moves are what make this a modeling task rather than a solving task.

Why this and not the rest: T2 (form conversion) is procedural and, we assume, already assessed
under supervision in the Unit 4 quiz — please correct us if this take-home is the only place
you see it. T5 (limitation) matters but is secondary; the redesign keeps it at reduced weight.
```

### 4 · One redesigned version

The task is the same. Two changes: a five-minute prediction on the day it is assigned, and the generic paragraph in Part 5 is removed to pay for it.

> **Algebra 2 — Unit 4 Take-Home Task**
> **Modeling with Quadratic Functions**
> Due: one week from today · 20 points · Individual work · Graphing calculator or Desmos allowed
>
> **In class today, before you start (Part 0 — initialled by me, required for credit):**
> Choose your situation (A, B or C — same three as before). On the front page of this packet, in pen, *without calculating anything*, answer:
> (a) Will the vertex be a highest point or a lowest point? What will it mean in your situation — what quantity is being maximised or minimised?
> (b) Roughly where do you expect the zeros to be, and what would a zero *mean* here? Is there one you'd throw out? Why?
> (c) Pick one number from your situation. Which coefficient do you expect it to turn into, and why?
> Hand me the packet; I'll initial and date it. **Turn this page in with your final work.**
>
> **Part 1 (4 pts).** Define your variables and write a quadratic function that models your situation. Explain where each number in your equation comes from.
>
> **Part 2 (4 pts).** Rewrite your function in a different form. State the vertex and the zeros. Show your work.
>
> **Part 3 (3 pts).** Graph your function (axes provided, or a labelled Desmos screenshot). Label vertex, intercepts, and a reasonable domain.
>
> **Part 4 (7 pts).** Interpret in context, and compare with your Part 0 predictions. What does the vertex tell you about your situation? What do the zeros mean? What is a reasonable domain, and why? **Then:** find at least one place where your Part 0 prediction was wrong or imprecise, and explain what in the mathematics changed your mind. (If you think all three were exactly right, explain what confirmed each one.)
>
> **Part 5 (2 pts).** Describe one specific limitation of your model — something real that the equation ignores.
>
> **Rubric**
>
> | Part | full | partial | 0–1 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 0. Prediction (0 pts, required) | Present, initialled | — | Missing → Part 4 capped at 3 |
> | 1. Model (4) | unchanged | unchanged | unchanged |
> | 2. Forms (4) | unchanged | unchanged | unchanged |
> | 3. Graph (3) | Accurate, labelled, sensible domain | Missing labels or domain | Inaccurate |
> | 4. Interpretation + comparison (7) | Vertex, zeros, domain explained in context (4); comparison names a specific prediction, says what was off, and ties it to the math (3) | Interpretation sound; comparison generic ("I was mostly right") | Interpretation missing or comparison absent |
> | 5. Limitation (2) | Specific and real | Vague | Missing |

> **What did not change.** The learning objective is the same: model a situation with a quadratic, attribute the coefficients, convert forms, interpret vertex/zeros/domain in context, name a limitation (T1–T5). The cognitive demand is the same band: a student who could earn full marks on the original does the same reasoning here, plus one act of comparison against their own prediction — which is reasoning the original already asked for implicitly in "what does the vertex tell you." The three situations, the tools allowed, the one-week window, and the 20-point total are unchanged. You can check this claim yourself: every rubric row in the original maps to exactly one row here; the only points moved are 1 from Part 3 and 1 from Part 5 into Part 4's comparison.

### 5 · One verification mechanism (and what it costs you)

```
Mechanism: prediction checkpoint (Part 0).
How it runs: on the day you assign it, students choose a situation and write three
  predictions in pen on the packet's front page — about five minutes. You walk the room and
  initial each page (a squiggle and the date; ~3 minutes for 30 students). You keep nothing.
  The initialled page comes back stapled to the final.
How it is reviewed: when grading Part 4, read the Part 0 page first (20 seconds), then check
  that the comparison paragraph names a real prediction from that page and says something true
  about why it changed. A comparison that doesn't match the page is the tell.

Cost, honestly (our estimate — please correct it after you run it once):
  Class time:   original 0 min per section      →  redesign 6–8 min per section
  Review time:  original ~5 min per student     →  redesign ~5 min per student
                (comparison paragraph adds ~1 min; dropping the Part 5 general paragraph saves
                 ~1 min; the graph is unchanged)
  Where the minutes came from: the 5–7-sentence "how quadratics help" paragraph is gone, and
                 Part 3 lost a point so there's less to adjudicate on the graph.
  Net for 5 sections × 28 students: about +35 class minutes per cycle, roughly 0 review
                 minutes. If your honest number is higher, tell us — it changes our advice.
```

### 6 · Why this is stronger

Your task was well designed for a world without these tools; here is what changed underneath it. Situations A–C are the ones a model has seen most, so the whole write-up — coefficients, forms, interpretation — now comes out of a text box in one turn. That is a fact about the tools, not your design.

The redesign anchors the evidence to one thing a tool cannot supply: what your student thought *before* they had an equation. A prediction made in class, in pen, on your initialled page, is theirs. Part 4 must reconcile the mathematics with it, and a mismatch shows in twenty seconds.

It costs about seven class minutes per section and no extra grading, because the generic paragraph is gone. It does not stop anyone. What survives is that their starting reasoning is on record and the comparison has to be *about* it.

Strongest argument against: it verifies the *start* of the thinking, not the middle. If Part 2's algebra matters here, this doesn't protect it.

This is one professional's read of one assignment. If you used it, tell us what it cost you to run — that answer shapes what we do next.

## Part C — Notes to Dalia (not sent)

**Does it fit two pages?** Not with the full redesigned task text and rubric in Section 4 — it runs to roughly 2.5 pages at 11-pt. Two options: (a) put the redesigned task on a separate attached page and keep only the diff ("Part 0 added, Part 5 trimmed, Part 4 reweighted") in Section 4; or (b) accept 2 + 1 attachment as the standard shape. I recommend (a) — the teacher wants the handout as a file anyway, not embedded in the audit. Decide before Phase 0.

**Why a prediction checkpoint and not the other three.** Selected-evidence doesn't fit (the situations are given, there's nothing to select). A worked-reasoning artifact (in-class first attempt at Part 1) would protect T1 more directly, but costs 10–15 class minutes and 1–2 review minutes each — for 140 students that is 2–4 hours of grading added per cycle, which is exactly the §11 failure. Defense questions are the strongest verification and the most expensive; 90 seconds × 140 students is 3.5 hours. The prediction checkpoint is the cheapest mechanism that reaches the invariant. It is a compromise and the audit says so.

**The honest state of the band judgment.** I have written "Checked by" with two options. I did not run the assignment through anything — I am the kind of thing it would be run through, and my saying "yes I could do this" is not an independent observation. Before Phase 1, paste Part A into two current models and grade the outputs against the rubric. If either scores under full marks on Part 1 or Part 4, the band is arguably 2 ("most of it, but not the coefficient attribution") and the whole audit reads differently. This ten-minute check should be part of every audit until you decide it never changes the answer. **Status: modeled.**

**The teacher-minute estimates are modeled.** "~5 min per student" for a 20-point modeling task with a graph is my guess from the rubric's size. The plan's evidence is 5–10 grading hours/week total, self-reported (observed), not per-assignment minutes. Phase 0's five dry-run teachers should each be asked for their real number on the original before we quote any net. The +35 class minutes is arithmetic on a modeled 7 minutes.

**The thing most likely to be wrong in Section 1.** T2. If this take-home is where the teacher actually assesses form conversion (some do; the quiz is procedural drill), then dropping it from the invariant is the correction they'll send — and that's fine; the redesign still protects T1/T4 and the rubric leaves T2 at 4 points. But it means our "assessed elsewhere" assumption should always be phrased as a question, which it is.

**Math-specific risk for the wedge hypothesis.** This audit was easy to write because Algebra 2 modeling tasks have a clean objective structure — the plan's stated advantage of the math wedge. The harder question the contrast cell answers is whether math teachers *feel* this as a problem. A teacher who assesses modeling mostly on the unit test may read this audit, agree, and not care, because the take-home is 20 points of low-stakes practice. Record that reaction if it comes; it is the signal the plan predicted.
