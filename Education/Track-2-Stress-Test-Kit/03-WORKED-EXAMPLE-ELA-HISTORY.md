# Worked example 2 — US History take-home essay (the contrast cell)

**Status:** draft for Dalia's approval · 2026-09-04. The assignment below is a typical 11th-grade US History argumentative essay with a document packet — the shape that generates most of the blue-book and oral-defence reaction in the evidence. It is not a real teacher's submission. It is chosen deliberately to exercise the **mixed copyright bucket** (teacher-authored prompt, publisher-derived documents), because that is the routing case most likely to come up in the ELA/history cell.

Part A: the assignment as written. Part B: the audit. Part C: notes to Dalia, including why this cell is expected to cost more teacher-minutes than math and what that means for the wedge question.

---

## Part A — The original assignment, as written by the teacher

> **US History — Unit 7 Take-Home Essay**
> **What caused the Great Depression?**
> Due: one week from today · 50 points · Individual work
>
> Historians disagree about whether the Great Depression was caused mainly by **long-term structural weaknesses** in the 1920s economy (uneven distribution of wealth, farm crisis, overproduction, weak banking system, buying on margin) or by the **immediate shock** of the October 1929 crash and the policy responses that followed it (the Federal Reserve's tightening, the Smoot-Hawley tariff, bank failures).
>
> Take a position. Using **at least three of the six documents** in the packet (Documents A–F, from the textbook's primary-source section) and your textbook, write an argumentative essay of **800–1,000 words** that:
> - opens with a clear thesis stating which category of cause was most significant and why;
> - has at least three body paragraphs, each built around evidence from a document, with the evidence explained (not just quoted);
> - includes a counterargument paragraph that fairly states the other view and responds to it;
> - ends with a conclusion that does more than repeat the thesis.
>
> Cite documents in parentheses: (Doc. C). MLA heading and formatting. Typed.
>
> **Documents in packet (from the textbook):**
> A. Excerpt, Herbert Hoover, "Rugged Individualism" speech, 1928
> B. Table: share of national income by top 1% / top 5%, 1920–1929
> C. Excerpt, Federal Reserve Bulletin on credit conditions, 1929
> D. Farm price index, 1919–1932 (graph)
> E. Excerpt, John Maynard Keynes on the tariff and world trade, 1930
> F. Photograph and caption: Bank run, New York, 1931
>
> **Rubric (50)**
>
> | Criterion | Points |
> |---|---|
> | Thesis: clear position on long-term vs. immediate, and why | 10 |
> | Evidence & analysis: 3+ documents used accurately and explained | 20 |
> | Counterargument: fairly stated and answered | 8 |
> | Organization | 6 |
> | Conventions (MLA, grammar, citations) | 6 |

### 2 · Where a current model can substitute for the evidence

```
T1 — The long-term / immediate framing is the standard historiographical split in every
     textbook. A model sorts causes into the two categories from the prompt alone.
T2 — A thesis in either direction, with "and why," is produced from the prompt.
T3 — Documents A–F are canonical (Hoover 1928, income-share table, Fed Bulletin, farm index,
     Keynes 1930, bank-run photo). A model produces accurate paraphrase and plausible
     quotation of most of them without the packet; with the packet pasted in, it produces
     three body paragraphs each "explaining, not just quoting" the document, with (Doc. C)
     citations in place.
T4 — A fair counterargument paragraph with a response is a standard model output.
T5 — MLA heading, 800–1,000 words, non-repetitive conclusion: produced.

Judgment: A current model can produce the whole artifact — because the position, the
categories, the document set and the essay structure are all specified in the prompt, and
the documents are the most widely reproduced sources on this topic. Nothing the student
submits depends on a choice or a piece of information that exists only with the student.
```

### 3 · What thinking must remain visible

If the redesign preserves nothing else, it must keep this visible:

```
The invariant: the student's own selection of evidence and their own warrant for it — which
document, sorted into which category of cause, supporting which position, and why (T1 + T3).
That selection-and-warrant move is the historical thinking; the essay is the container.

Why this and not the rest: T2 (thesis) and T4 (counterargument) are important but follow from
the selection — a student who has genuinely chosen and warranted two documents can be asked
to build the thesis and counterargument on them. T5 (organisation, conventions) is a
writing-skills target that this essay shares with every other essay this year; we lower its
weight rather than protect it here. Please correct us if this is the essay where you assess
MLA form.
```

### 4 · One redesigned version

The task is the same. Two changes: a twelve-minute evidence-card step in class on the day it is assigned, and a shorter essay with reduced formatting requirements to pay for it.

> **US History — Unit 7 Take-Home Essay**
> **What caused the Great Depression?**
> Due: one week from today · 50 points · Individual work
>
> *(Prompt paragraph unchanged: long-term structural vs. immediate shock, as above.)*
>
> **In class today, before you write (Evidence Card — initialled by me, required for credit):**
> Read the packet. On the card, in pen, *without your textbook or any device*:
> 1. Which way are you leaning — long-term or immediate? (You may change your mind later. If you do, say so in the essay and explain what changed it.)
> 2. Choose **two** documents. For each, write: its letter; which category of cause it belongs to and *why you sorted it there*; **one phrase copied exactly** from the document, in quotation marks; and one sentence on how that phrase supports the position you're leaning toward.
> Hand me the card; I'll initial and date it and hand it straight back. **Staple it to your essay.**
>
> **The essay (600–750 words):**
> - a clear thesis stating which category of cause was most significant and why;
> - at least three body paragraphs, each built around evidence from a document, with the evidence explained (not just quoted). **Two of the three must be the documents on your card, and each must use the exact phrase you copied.** The third document is your choice.
> - a counterargument paragraph that fairly states the other view and responds to it;
> - a conclusion that does more than repeat the thesis.
>
> Cite documents in parentheses: (Doc. C). Typed. *(No MLA heading required — name, date, period at the top is fine.)*
>
> **Rubric (50)**
>
> | Criterion | Points | Change |
> |---|---|---|
> | Thesis: clear position on long-term vs. immediate, and why | 10 | — |
> | Evidence & analysis: 3+ documents used accurately and explained | 20 | — |
> | Card consistency: the two card documents appear with the copied phrase; the essay's sorting and warrant for each match the card, or the essay explicitly says what changed and why | 6 | new (from Conventions 4, Organization 2) |
> | Counterargument: fairly stated and answered | 8 | — |
> | Organization | 4 | −2 |
> | Conventions (grammar, citations) | 2 | −4; MLA dropped |

> **What did not change.** The learning objective is the same: distinguish long-term from immediate causes, take a position, select and explain documentary evidence, answer a counterargument, write it as an argument (T1–T5). The cognitive demand is the same band: a student who could earn full marks on the original has to do the same historical reasoning here — sorting, selecting, warranting, arguing — with the selection now committed in writing before drafting rather than reconstructed afterwards. The prompt, the two categories, the six documents, the three-document minimum, the counterargument requirement, the one-week window and the 50-point total are unchanged. The essay is shorter (600–750 vs. 800–1,000 words); length was never a rubric criterion, and the analysis demand per paragraph is identical. You can check this claim yourself: every original rubric row is present; the six new points come only from Organization and Conventions.

### 5 · One verification mechanism (and what it costs you)

```
Mechanism: selected-evidence step (the Evidence Card).
How it runs: on assignment day, students read the packet and fill the card in pen, no
  devices — about 12 minutes including your initialling pass (a squiggle and date; ~3 min for
  30 students). Cards go back to students immediately; you keep nothing. Card comes back
  stapled to the essay.
How it is reviewed: read the card first (~30 seconds). While grading Evidence & Analysis,
  check that the two card documents are the ones used, that the copied phrase appears, and
  that the sorting/warrant in the essay matches the card or the essay names the change. A
  polished essay that quietly swaps documents or contradicts the card's warrant is the tell.

Cost, honestly (our estimate — please correct it after you run it once):
  Class time:   original 0 min per section      →  redesign ~12 min per section
  Review time:  original ~11 min per essay      →  redesign ~10 min per essay
                (card check adds ~1 min; a 600–750-word essay reads ~2 min faster than
                 800–1,000; MLA checking gone saves a little)
  Where the minutes came from: length cap lowered by ~25%; MLA formatting removed; rubric
                 row for Organization narrowed.
  Net for 5 sections × 28 students: about +60 class minutes per cycle; review roughly
                 −2 hours per cycle if the length cut holds. If your essays already ran
                 short, expect review time to be neutral, not lower.
```

### 6 · Why this is stronger

Your essay was well designed for a world without these tools; here is what changed underneath it. The prompt names the positions, the packet supplies the best-known sources, and the structure is spelled out — which makes it a good assignment, and gives a model everything it needs.

The redesign anchors the evidence to what was always the real thinking: which documents a student chooses, how they sort them, and why. That choice is now made in class, in pen, on a card you initial. The essay is built on it — same documents, same phrases, same warrant, or an honest account of what changed. A mismatch shows in thirty seconds.

It costs about twelve class minutes and, with a shorter essay, slightly less grading. It does not stop anyone; a student can photograph the card for a model. What survives is that the selection and warrant are the student's own.

Strongest argument against: it locks in a first impression; the "say what changed" clause helps, but asks more of weaker writers.

This is one professional's read of one assignment. If you used it, tell us what it cost you to run — that answer shapes what we do next.

## Part C — Notes to Dalia (not sent)

**Why this cell costs more, and what that means for the wedge.** The plan's hypothesis is that acute pain clusters in writing-heavy subjects. This example shows the other half of that: the *cost* also clusters there. An essay takes ~10 minutes to grade against ~5 for a math modeling task, and any verification adds to a larger base. Here the redesign only nets out neutral-or-better because we cut length and MLA — which some teachers will refuse ("800 words is the district expectation"). If a history teacher rejects the length cut, the honest net is roughly +1 minute per essay, +2.5 hours per cycle for 140 students, which is precisely the §11 failure. **Record whether contrast-cell teachers accept or reject the subtraction.** That single data point tells you whether the "pay for the mechanism by removing something" principle survives contact with writing teachers.

**The mixed bucket is the normal case in this cell, not the exception.** Nearly every DBQ-style task uses publisher or College Board documents under a teacher-authored prompt. The audit above shows the routing: the prompt and rubric are corpus-eligible (teacher-authored), the documents are analysed for the teacher only and never reproduced. The submission form (04) needs to make "mixed" a first-class option with a follow-up ("which parts are yours?"), or the bucket data will be garbage. I've put that in.

**The band judgment is modeled, same caveat as 02.** I did not run this through anything. For DBQ-style essays my confidence that a model produces a full-marks artifact is higher than for the math task — but that is still a reviewer's judgment, and the "Checked by" line should say which it was.

**Why selected-evidence and not defense questions.** Defense questions (2–3 questions about the student's own essay, 90 seconds each) are the strongest verification for essays and are what the 14% of teachers already doing oral defence use. For 140 students it is ~3.5 hours per cycle of teacher time that no subtraction can pay for. The Evidence Card gets most of the same information (does this student know why they chose Doc C?) at 30 seconds of review. If the teacher is one of the 14%, offer the defence-question variant as the follow-up *after* they've used this — but not in the audit, which ships one mechanism.

**Equity note worth carrying into Section 6 if the teacher raises it.** The Evidence Card is a 12-minute in-class, pen-on-paper step — the same modality as the blue-book workarounds the evidence calls equity-hostile. The difference is scope: it is a card with two quotations and four sentences, not a timed essay, and the essay itself stays typed, untimed, at home. Multilingual students and students with accommodations can be given the packet the day before, or the card can be dictated. Say this only if asked; it's not in the 200 words.
