## Part A — The original assignment (unchanged from v1)

> **Algebra 2 — Unit 4 Take-Home Task · Modeling with Quadratic Functions**
> Due: one week from today · 20 points · Individual work · Graphing calculator or Desmos allowed
>
> Choose **one**: **A. Projectile** — water balloon launched upward from a 12-ft platform at 40 ft/s (*h(t) = −16t² + v₀t + h₀*). **B. Revenue** — spirit shirts: at $10 they sell 200; each $1 increase sells 10 fewer. **C. Area** — 60 m of fencing, rectangular pen against a barn wall (three sides fenced).
>
> **Part 1 (4).** Define variables, write the quadratic, explain where each number comes from. **Part 2 (4).** Rewrite in another form; state vertex and zeros; show work. **Part 3 (4).** Graph; label vertex, intercepts, reasonable domain. **Part 4 (5).** Interpret in context: vertex, zeros, domain and why. **Part 5 (3).** Paragraph on how quadratics help solve such problems; one limitation of the model.
>
> Rubric as in v1 (full / partial / 0–1 per part).

### 3 · The verification moment

```
The item — "One Thing Changed" (full sheet and six keys attached; variant A2 shown):

  You modelled the water balloon: h(t) = −16t² + 40t + 12.
  Now the platform is 20 feet tall instead of 12. Everything else is the same.
  WITHOUT calculating:
    1. Which number(s) in your function change, and which stay the same? Say why, in terms
       of the balloon.
    2. Does the highest point move — up, down, earlier, later, or not at all? What does that
       mean for the balloon?
    3. Does the time it lands change? Earlier or later? Why?
  If you have time: check ONE of your three answers by calculating.

Variant rule: two variants per situation (A: platform 20 ft / launch speed 60 ft/s;
  B: 15 fewer shirts per $1 / start at 250 shirts; C: 80 m of fencing / a fourth side needed).
  Six versions; each student gets one for the situation they chose, assigned by row.
  Next cycle, different variants — the change is a number, so new versions cost minutes.
Conditions: in class on the day the project is due, before collection. 6 minutes, written,
  in pen, the student's own project face-down on the desk, no device.
Coverage: all students, every cycle.

Sufficient evidence — a "yes" answer contains:
  C1 (Q1): names the changed coefficient (the constant term / v₀ / the 60) and ties it to the
           feature (platform height / speed / fencing length); says the others don't change.
  C2 (Q2): correct direction of the vertex AND what it means (higher max / same time; later
           and higher max; smaller max revenue at a lower price; bigger max area).
  C3 (Q3): correct direction of the relevant zero AND its meaning (lands later; breaks even
           at a different price; larger possible width).
  Threshold: Q1 and Q2 both with a stated reason → yes. Q3 alone → no. Reason: C1 and C2 are
           the modelling claims; C3 follows from them and is the easiest to guess.

Delegation test on the moment itself:
  Could it be pre-prepared? A student could ask a model, before class, "for situation A, what
  happens to the vertex and zeros if the platform height or launch speed changes?" and
  memorise the answer. A student who did that and can reproduce it on a variant they haven't
  seen, without notes, has done the thing C1 and C2 claim. We are fine with that.
  Residual risk: a phone during six supervised minutes; a student who guesses directions
  without reasons (the "stated reason" requirement is what catches this).

Cost, honestly (our estimate — correct it after one run):
  To prepare:   ~15 min once — the six keys are in the attachment; you check them.
  Class time:   6–8 min per section (6 on the clock, ~2 to hand out and collect).
  To score:     ~1 min per student — three lines against a key, tick/no-tick plus reason.
  Per cycle, 5 sections × 28 students:  +35–40 class min, +2 h 20 min scoring
     — IF the project is still graded in full as before.
  Per cycle if the item carries the grade and the project is checked for completion
  (~30 s each) instead: scoring drops from ~5 min to ~1.5 min per student — about 8 hours
  LESS per cycle than today. Which of those two lines applies to you is Section 4.
```

### 4 · What the original task becomes, and the gradebook question

> **What did not change.** Your task is unchanged: same three situations, same five parts, same tools, same week. What changed is which piece of evidence carries the claim. The take-home is where students practise modelling and produce something they can be proud of — with whatever tool policy you choose; the six minutes on due day is where you see for yourself whether C1–C3 happened. Nothing about the task got harder or easier for a tool, and we are not asking you to police whether one was used.

> This audit assumes the six-minute item can carry the grade weight the project used to carry, and the project can be checked for completion rather than graded in full. **Is that a decision you can make this semester without anyone's sign-off?** If not, tell us who owns it — a chair, a department vote, a district grading policy. That answer matters to us more than whether you like the audit.

### 5 · Why this and not a redesign

Your task was well designed for a world without these tools; here is what changed underneath it. The three situations are the ones a model has seen most, so the whole write-up now comes out of a text box in one turn. That is a fact about the tools, not your design.

Our first instinct was to keep the thinking visible — a prediction on the day it's assigned, initialled, reconciled in the final. We dropped it. A student can photograph that page and ask for an essay consistent with it. Any checkpoint that leaves the room is a prompt.

So the evidence moved to six minutes in the room. One number in the situation changes; the student says what moves and why, before calculating. It is a version they haven't seen and it can't be handed over. It costs about seven class minutes and a minute a student to score — or saves you hours, if it can carry the grade and the project becomes practice.

It does not detect anything. It does not tell you who typed the project. It tells you whether the student can do what the project claimed.

Strongest argument against: it is a quiz. What we're claiming is that *this* quiz, on *this* claim, is enough — and we don't know that yet.

This is one professional's read of one assignment. If you ran the six minutes, tell us what it cost and whether it told you anything the project didn't — those two answers shape what we do next.

## Part C — Attachment: item sheet and keys

**Student sheet (one per variant; the three questions are identical, only the change differs):**

| Variant | Situation | The one change | Base function |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Projectile | Launch speed 60 ft/s instead of 40 | h = −16t² + 40t + 12 |
| A2 | Projectile | Platform 20 ft instead of 12 | h = −16t² + 40t + 12 |
| B1 | Revenue | 15 fewer shirts per $1 instead of 10 | R = (10 + x)(200 − 10x) |
| B2 | Revenue | 250 shirts at $10 instead of 200 | R = (10 + x)(200 − 10x) |
| C1 | Area | 80 m of fencing instead of 60 | A = x(60 − 2x) |
| C2 | Area | No barn — all four sides fenced, 60 m | A = x(60 − 2x) |

**Keys (teacher only):**

| Variant | Q1 — what changes | Q2 — vertex | Q3 — zero |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Only the *t* coefficient (40→60); −16 is gravity, 12 is the platform | Later (t = 1.25→1.875 s) and higher (37→68.25 ft) | Lands later (≈2.77→≈3.94 s) |
| A2 | Only the constant (12→20); it's where the balloon starts | Same time (1.25 s), higher (37→45 ft) | Lands later (≈2.77→≈2.93 s) |
| B1 | The "10 fewer" becomes 15: R = (10+x)(200−15x); the 10 and 200 stay | Best price lower ($15→≈$11.67), max revenue lower ($2250→≈$2042) | Revenue hits zero at a lower price ($30→≈$23.33) |
| B2 | The 200 becomes 250; the $10 and "10 fewer" stay | Best price higher ($15→$17.50), max revenue higher ($2250→$3062.50) | Zero revenue at a higher price ($30→$35) |
| C1 | The 60 becomes 80: A = x(80−2x); the 2 stays (still two fenced widths) | Best width larger (15→20 m), max area larger (450→800 m²) | Largest possible width larger (30→40 m) |
| C2 | Length is now 30−x, not 60−2x: A = x(30−x); the change is in *what the length expression is* | Best width 15 m (unchanged), max area smaller (450→225 m²) | Largest possible width 30 m (unchanged) |

C2 is the hardest variant and the best diagnostic: a student who says "the 60 changes" has not attributed the 2. Use it for students whose projects were strongest.

## Part D — Notes to Dalia (not sent)

**The number this whole experiment is now about.** For each student: project grade (full / partial / low) crossed with item result (yes / no). The cell that matters is *full marks on the project, no on the item*. If it is empty across five classes, the delegation problem may be real but the evidence problem is not, and the article changes. If it is 20–40% of the class, that is the existence proof, and it is the honest opening line of the Substack piece. Record it as a count per section, never per student, and never to us with names.

**Why written-to-all and not oral-to-a-sample.** Ninety seconds × 140 students is 3.5 hours of the teacher's mouth; six minutes × 5 sections is 35 minutes of the clock. Written also removes the equity problem oral defence shares with blue books. The oral version is the fallback when a claim can't be reduced to three lines — that is the ELA/history situation, and it's why math is the wedge.

**The sampling alternative, if six minutes isn't there.** Eight students per cycle, ninety seconds each at the desk while the class works, rotating so everyone is verified twice a semester. Cheaper in class minutes, more expensive in fairness questions ("why me?") and in gradebook design. Not the default; record it if a teacher asks for it.

**The eight-hours-saved line is the one to be most careful with.** It is true arithmetic on a modeled 5 min/student, and it depends entirely on the gradebook answer in Section 4. If the teacher must still grade the project in full, the honest net is +2.3 h per cycle — the §11 failure, now stated rather than hidden. Do not lead with the savings in outreach until Phase 0 has a real number.

**"It's a quiz."** It is. The reply is in Section 5's last paragraph and it should stay there, unhedged. What is new is not the item; it is (a) generating it from the claim rather than the content, (b) the variant rule, (c) the pre-stated sufficiency line, and (d) the question of how few of these a semester needs. Phase 0 tests (a)–(c). Phase 1 is (d).

**What is modeled here.** Every minute figure; the 20–40% guess for the evidence gap; the assumption that a Part 2 quiz exists. What is observed: that A–C are canonical (any textbook); that variants cost minutes to produce (the table above took about that long).
