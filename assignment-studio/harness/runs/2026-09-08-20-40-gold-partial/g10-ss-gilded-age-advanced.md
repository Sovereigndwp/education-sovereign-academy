## NEW VERSION

# Two Views of Wealth in the Gilded Age — Document Analysis

**Grade 10 U.S. History · Industrialization Unit · 45 minutes**

Read both documents. Answer the questions in Part A, then write the response in Part B.

---

**Document 1 — Andrew Carnegie, "Wealth," *North American Review*, June 1889 (excerpt)**

> The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth, so that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship. The conditions of human life have not only been changed, but revolutionized, within the past few hundred years. … The contrast between the palace of the millionaire and the cottage of the laborer with us to-day measures the change which has come with civilization. This change, however, is not to be deplored, but welcomed as highly beneficial. … Much better this great irregularity than universal squalor.

**Document 2 — Preamble to the Omaha Platform of the People's (Populist) Party, July 1892 (excerpt)**

> We meet in the midst of a nation brought to the verge of moral, political, and material ruin. Corruption dominates the ballot-box, the Legislatures, the Congress, and touches even the ermine of the bench. … The fruits of the toil of millions are boldly stolen to build up colossal fortunes for a few, unprecedented in the history of mankind; and the possessors of these, in turn, despise the Republic and endanger liberty. From the same prolific womb of governmental injustice we breed the two great classes — tramps and millionaires.

---

### Part A — Sourcing and close reading

1. For each document, identify the **author**, the **year**, and the **audience** the author was most likely trying to reach. Then add one sentence explaining **why** that audience matters to understanding what the author chose to say or leave out.

2. Both authors describe the same fact: a large gap between the very rich and everyone else. In one sentence each, state how **Document 1** explains where that gap came from, and how **Document 2** explains where it came from. Then write one additional sentence: **What does each author assume his audience already believes about who deserves wealth?**

3. Choose one word or phrase from each document that shows the author's attitude toward great fortunes. Explain what the word choice reveals. Then: **If each author had been writing for the *other's* audience, what word might he have chosen instead, and why?**

4. Why might Carnegie, of all people, have written Document 1? Why might a farmers' and workers' party have written Document 2? Use what you know about each author's position in 1889–1892. Then: **What might each author have been trying to prevent his audience from believing or doing?**

### Part B — Argument

5. **Question:** In the late nineteenth century, did Americans see the growing gap between rich and poor as the natural price of progress, or as evidence that the system had failed?

Write a response of two paragraphs that:
- takes a position that accounts for **both** documents and explains **why different groups reached opposite conclusions from the same economic facts** (what interests, experiences, or beliefs led each to interpret wealth inequality as they did?);
- uses at least one piece of evidence from **each** document;
- explains how the **point of view** of each author affects how much weight a historian should give their claim **when trying to understand what most Americans in each group actually thought** (not just what leaders said);
- connects the documents to at least one specific development from the period (for example: the growth of trusts, the Homestead or Pullman strikes, farm debt, the 1893 depression) and explains **how that development would have reinforced or challenged each author's explanation of the wealth gap**.

**Scoring (6 points):** Sourcing accurate and audience significance explained (1) · Position explains why groups reached different conclusions (1) · Evidence from each document (1) · Point of view used to weigh reliability for understanding group beliefs (1) · Outside development connected accurately with explanation of how it shaped interpretations (1) · Organization and clarity (1).
