# Author's Point of View — Evidence Paragraph

**Grade 8 English Language Arts · Informational Text · One class period**

Read the passage. Then complete the task below.

---

### The Bridge on Mill Street

The Mill Street bridge is ninety-one years old, and for most of those years nobody thought about it at all. It carried farm trucks, then school buses, then the commuters who now cross it twice a day without looking down. Last spring an inspection report gave it a rating of "poor," and the town council scheduled a vote on whether to repair it or replace it with a wider concrete span.

The case for replacement is simple, and the council's engineer made it in eleven minutes. The steel is tired. The deck is narrow enough that two delivery vans cannot pass without one of them slowing to a crawl. Repair would cost about two-thirds of what a new bridge costs and would buy, in the engineer's estimate, twenty more years. A new bridge would last seventy-five.

What the engineer did not mention is that the Mill Street bridge is the only place in town where you can stand over moving water. Children drop leaves from the upstream rail and run across to watch them come out the other side. Every graduating class since 1974 has taken its photograph on it. The wider concrete span in the proposal has no sidewalk on the upstream side at all.

None of this appears in the inspection report, because inspection reports measure steel. They do not measure what a town does on a bridge, or what it would stop doing if the bridge became a road that happens to cross a river. The council will vote in March. The numbers are on one side. I am not sure the numbers are the only thing that should be counted.

---

### Task

In one well-developed paragraph (8–12 sentences), answer this question:

**What is the author's point of view on replacing the Mill Street bridge, and how does the author respond to the opposing case?**

Your paragraph must:

- State the author's point of view in your own words (do not just say "the author is against it").
- Quote or closely paraphrase **two** pieces of textual evidence that reveal the author's point of view.
- For each piece of evidence, explain **how** it shows the author's position (word choice, contrast, placement, or what the author chooses to include).
- Explain how the author **acknowledges and responds to** the engineer's case for replacement.

**Scoring (4-point rubric):** Point of view clearly stated and accurate (1) · Two relevant pieces of evidence (1) · Explanation connects each piece of evidence to the point of view (1) · Accurately describes how the author handles the opposing view (1).


## Gold Learning Contract

- **Learning Target:** An author's point of view in an informational text is revealed through choices — what is included and omitted, word choice, framing and selected details, contrast, and the order and placement of ideas — and a skilled author acknowledges the opposing case rather than ignoring it.
- **Required Thinking:** Infer an unstated-but-signalled position from the text; select evidence that reveals the position (not merely mentions the topic); explain the mechanism by which each piece of evidence signals the position (word choice, framing/selected detail, contrast, inclusion/omission, placement); describe how the author treats the counter-case (acknowledge, concede, respond).
- **Required Evidence:** One paragraph containing: an accurate, specific statement of the author's position in the student's words; two quotations/close paraphrases; an explanation for each that names a technique (word choice, framing/selected detail, contrast, inclusion/omission, placement); a sentence on how the engineer's case is acknowledged and answered.
- **Teacher Constraints:** One class period; single paragraph of 8–12 sentences; graded with the stated 4-point rubric; the rubric is the same for every student.

**Reviewer note:** The passage deliberately never says 'I think the bridge should be repaired' — the position must be inferred from technique. That inference is the construct. Any version that states the position for the student, or reduces the task to locating two quotes, has broken the contract. The single 8–12 sentence paragraph is an explicit teacher constraint (and the rubric is applied to it), so the paragraph is preserved as a constraint, not because the rubric requires a paragraph.

## Answer key

- **point_of_view:** The author favours keeping/repairing the bridge — or at least doubts that cost and engineering data alone should decide — because the bridge's civic and human uses are real but uncounted.
- **strong_evidence_examples:** ["'the only place in town where you can stand over moving water' — inclusion of what the engineer omitted", "'inspection reports measure steel. They do not measure what a town does on a bridge' — contrast of measured vs unmeasured", "'made it in eleven minutes' — framing / selected detail that minimises the opposing case", "'I am not sure the numbers are the only thing that should be counted' — direct, hedged statement placed last", "'a road that happens to cross a river' — diminishing description of the replacement"]

# CASE `g8-sci-ramp-data` — Grade 8 · Science · data interpretation + CER (MS-PS3-1 / MS-PS3-5)

## The assignment (as a teacher would upload it)

# Ramp Height and Cart Speed — Data Analysis (CER)

**Grade 8 Physical Science · Energy Unit · 30 minutes**

A class released a cart from rest at four different heights on the same ramp and measured its speed at the bottom with a photogate. Each height was tested three times.

| Release height (cm) | Trial 1 speed (m/s) | Trial 2 speed (m/s) | Trial 3 speed (m/s) | Average speed (m/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1.28 | 1.33 | 1.30 | |
| 20 | 1.84 | 1.87 | 1.82 | |
| 30 | 2.24 | 2.28 | 2.23 | |
| 40 | 2.58 | 2.62 | 2.61 | |

### Part 1 — Organize the data

1. Calculate the average speed for each height and fill in the last column. Round to the nearest hundredth.

2. Sketch a graph of average speed (y-axis) versus release height (x-axis). Label both axes with units.

### Part 2 — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

3. **Claim.** Write one sentence stating how the cart's speed at the bottom is related to its release height.

4. **Evidence.** Support your claim with at least two specific numbers from the table. Say what happens to speed when the height doubles from 10 cm to 20 cm, and from 20 cm to 40 cm.

5. **Reasoning.** Explain *why* the relationship looks the way it does. Use the ideas of gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy in your explanation.

### Part 3 — Apply

6. A student says, "If we release the cart from 80 cm, it will go twice as fast as from 40 cm." Do you agree? Use the pattern in the data to explain.

7. Predict the average speed for a release height of 50 cm. Explain how you made your prediction.


## Gold Learning Contract

- **Learning Target:** Releasing the cart from higher up gives it more gravitational potential energy, which is transferred to kinetic energy on the way down (all of it in an ideal frictionless model; most of it in the real cart). Because kinetic energy depends on speed squared, speed grows roughly with the square root of release height — it increases with height but not proportionally to it.
- **Required Thinking:** Compute and graph averages; identify a non-linear pattern from the numbers (compare ratios, not just 'it goes up'); construct a claim supported by specific data; explain the pattern with an energy-transfer model; use the pattern to evaluate a proportional-reasoning misconception and to extrapolate a prediction just beyond the measured range, stating the method.
- **Required Evidence:** Correct averages; a labelled graph; a claim sentence; at least two quoted data comparisons including the two doublings; reasoning that names GPE→KE transfer and the speed-squared relationship in some grade-appropriate form; an explicit disagreement with the 'twice as fast' claim backed by the ×1.4 pattern; a prediction near 2.9 m/s for 50 cm with a stated method (extrapolation).
- **Teacher Constraints:** 30 minutes; paper; graph sketched by hand; CER format for Part 2.

**Reviewer note:** The difficult part is item 6 — recognising that the data contradict proportional reasoning. Item 7 is extrapolation (50 cm is beyond the measured 10–40 cm), and the method matters more than the number. A Support version that changes the data to be linear, or tells students 'notice speed does not double', has removed the construct. Items 1–2 are procedural on purpose; Support may reduce them (e.g. pre-fill two of four averages) without touching the CER. Correctness gate: any regenerated numbers must be recomputed and stay consistent with the √h pattern; any energy statement must not claim all GPE becomes KE in the real cart without naming the ideal model.

## Answer key

- **1:** Averages: 10 cm → 1.30; 20 cm → 1.84; 30 cm → 2.25; 40 cm → 2.60 m/s.
- **2:** Curve rising and flattening (concave down), not a straight line through the origin.
- **3:** Speed increases with release height, but less than proportionally (doubling height does not double speed).
- **4:** 10→20 cm: 1.30→1.84 (×≈1.4, not ×2); 20→40 cm: 1.84→2.60 (×≈1.4, not ×2).
- **5:** Higher release → more gravitational potential energy (GPE ∝ height) → more of it is transferred to kinetic energy at the bottom (in an ideal, frictionless model all of it; in the real cart a roughly constant fraction is lost to friction and air resistance). Because KE depends on speed squared, doubling the energy multiplies speed by about √2 ≈ 1.4, not 2. (Grade-8 acceptable: 'the energy doubles but the speed does not, because kinetic energy grows faster than speed'.)
- **6:** Disagree. Pattern shows ×≈1.4 per doubling; 80 cm ≈ 2.60 × 1.4 ≈ 3.7 m/s, not 5.2.
- **7:** ≈ 2.9 m/s by extrapolation just beyond the measured range (accept 2.85–2.95 with a method: √(50/40)·2.60 ≈ 2.91, or extending a smooth curve; a straight-line extension giving ≈2.95 is acceptable if the student notes the curve is flattening). Credit the stated method and the acknowledgement that 50 cm lies outside the data.

## Boundary — SUPPORT

*Pass looks like*
- Averages partially pre-filled (e.g. 10 cm and 30 cm) so students still practise the computation but spend time on the CER; graph axes pre-drawn and scaled.
- CER sentence frames that structure ('When the height went from ___ to ___, the speed went from ___ to ___, which is ___ times as fast') without supplying the ×1.4 conclusion.
- Vocabulary glossed: potential energy, kinetic energy, photogate, proportional.

*Violation looks like*

- Item 6 reduced to yes/no with no explanation, or item 5 reduced to 'name two kinds of energy'.

## Boundary — ADVANCED

**Document 1 — Andrew Carnegie, "Wealth," *North American Review*, June 1889 (excerpt)**

## Answer key

- **1:** Doc 1: Andrew Carnegie, 1889, educated/elite readers of a national review (and, implicitly, fellow industrialists). Doc 2: People's Party (Populist) convention, 1892, farmers, laborers, and voters — a platform is a public appeal.
- **2:** Doc 1: the gap is the natural, beneficial by-product of industrial progress ('civilization'); Doc 2: the gap is produced by injustice and corruption — fortunes are 'stolen' through a captured government.
- **3:** Doc 1 examples: 'not to be deplored, but welcomed', 'irregularity' (a neutral word for inequality), 'highly beneficial'. Doc 2 examples: 'stolen', 'colossal', 'despise the Republic', 'tramps and millionaires' (pairing implies one produces the other).
- **4:** Carnegie: a steel magnate justifying his own fortune while proposing that the rich administer wealth for the public good (the 'Gospel of Wealth'); Populists: a party of indebted farmers and workers seeking government action (railroad regulation, silver, income tax) — the platform's purpose is to indict the system.
- **5:** Any defensible position that uses both documents, weighs each by POV (Carnegie's self-interest; the platform's political purpose), and connects to a period development accurately (e.g. Homestead 1892, Pullman 1894, Panic of 1893, trusts / Sherman Act 1890, farm debt and deflation).
