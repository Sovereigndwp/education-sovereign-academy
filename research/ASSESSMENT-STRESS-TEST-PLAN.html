2 · What remains painful despite all of it

This is where the evidence is strongest — and notably it comes from teacher behavior, not vendor claims.

  • Teachers are already doing the redesign, manually and at scale. Over 40% of secondary teachers already ask students for evidence of their process, and 14% already require an oral defence of submitted work. That is not a hypothetical need — it's an existing, unsupported workflow.
  • The substitution is confirmed and specific. 80% of lower-secondary and 73% of senior-secondary teachers who report student AI use say students use it to complete assessment tasks.
  • Teachers know they lack a method. About half of secondary teachers say they don't know how to prevent AI-related cheating.
  • The current workarounds are crude and equity-hostile. Blue-book sales up 30%+ at Texas A&M and ~50% at Florida; lockdown browsers; all writing moved in-class. But researchers note multilingual writers and students with disabilities are badly disadvantaged by timed handwritten conditions — and that the "AI-resistant classroom is a myth."
  • The measurement framing is now academic consensus. Stanford's Accelerator for Learning with ETS warns final-product assessments "may no longer capture the knowledge and skills they intend to measure"; a 2026 arXiv paper frames AI misuse explicitly as a measurement problem. A student who delegates to AI and one who learns through AI dialogue produce identical outputs.
The gap, stated precisely. Detection tells you whether AI was used. Generation tools make more materials. Assessment platforms deliver and score. Consultants run generic workshops. Nobody hands a specific teacher a specific redesign of their specific assignment that preserves what they were trying to measure. The 40% already asking for process evidence are inventing it themselves, one assignment at a time, with no method and no time.

3 · The strongest existing product to improve rather than reinvent

Assessment-design professional development. Not Turnitin, not Brisk — the PD/consulting product that Title II already funds at $2.19B/yr, that 78% of districts already buy, and whose spending is rising faster than any other category.

Why it's the right thing to improve

  • It's already bought, by a known buyer, from a protected budget, with no procurement cycle for software.
  • It's weak in a specific, fixable way: today it's slideware and generic frameworks. A teacher leaves a workshop with principles and no changed assignment.
  • Our version substitutes an artifact for a slide deck: every participating teacher leaves with their own assessments audited and redesigned. That is a categorical quality improvement in a product schools already pay $150/hr for.
  • It satisfies the new buying condition — districts must tie PD to measurable instructional outcomes, and a before/after map of what each assessment now proves is that evidence.

Runner-up worth noting: Turnitin's own "learning integrity" push is the closest competitive product, but it is higher-ed-rooted, detection-branded, and operates on student submissions. We operate on the assignment — no student data, no LMS. That difference is the wedge, and it should be stated in exactly those terms.

4 · Where a small entrant gets paid before LMS, student data, or district procurement

  1. Department-level PD sprint (Title II or building PD funds) — a department chair or C&I director can often authorize this without board procurement. Primary path.
  2. Summer institutes and in-service days — existing calendar slots with existing budget.
  3. Conference sessions / workshops — low revenue, high sourcing value; the room is the recruiting channel.
  4. Individual teacher purchases — small, but validates willingness before any institution is involved.
  5. Grants / foundation-funded pilots — plausible given the Stanford/ETS framing, slower.

All five avoid the two things that would sink a small entrant: student-data agreements and IT procurement.

5 · Free deliverable specification — the Assessment Stress Test

Input: one assignment, assessment, or task. Never student work, never student PII. Output: a single 2-page artifact, human-reviewed, returned in 48–72 hours.

1
What this assignment is trying to measure. The intended learning and reasoning, reconstructed from the task itself and stated as observable claims ("distinguish long-term from immediate causes"; "justify a choice of representation"). Written so the teacher can say yes, that's what I meant — or correct us.
2
Where AI can substitute for the evidence. Target by target, what a current model can produce without the student doing the thinking — quoting the assignment's own task verbs. Specific, not generic.
3
What thinking must remain visible. The cognitive-demand invariant: the reasoning that, if it disappears, means the assessment no longer measures the objective.
4
One redesigned version. Preserves the original learning objective and cognitive-demand band. Ships with an explicit "what did not change" statement — the preservation claim, auditable by the teacher.
5
One verification mechanism. Exactly one, chosen to fit the teacher's constraints: a prediction checkpoint, a selected-evidence step, a worked-reasoning artifact, or 2–3 defense questions generated from the task. One, not a menu — a menu is a workshop, not a deliverable.
6
Teacher-facing rationale, ≤200 words. Why the redesign is stronger, in plain professional language, addressed to a colleague — never to a novice.
No numerical AI-proof score. We cannot yet defend a number, so we will not invent one. Instead the substitution finding is a named qualitative judgment with its reason attached, in three bands: "a current model can produce the whole artifact" / "can produce most of it, but not [named step]" / "can assist, but the evidence of thinking survives because [reason]." Every band cites the specific task element that drives it, and the artifact says plainly that this is a professional judgment, not a measurement. If we later earn a defensible metric, it will come from the corpus — not from marketing.

Tone rule (this matters more than it looks)

The audit must never imply the teacher's assessment was bad. The framing is always "this task was well designed for a world without these tools; here is what changed underneath it." Telling a professional their assessment is invalid is the fastest way to lose them — and it's also untrue: the world moved, not their judgment.

6 · The exact first-100-teacher experiment

Wedge subject

Secondary math, as instructed — but with a deliberate 20-teacher contrast cell in ELA/history. My honest read of the evidence: the acute pain clusters in writing-heavy subjects (essays, blue books, oral defence all cluster there), so math may show lower baseline pain. That is exactly why the contrast cell exists — it costs little and tells us early whether math is the right beachhead or whether your credibility as a math teacher is pulling us toward the harder market. Math's compensating advantages are real: your network and credibility, cleaner objective/cognitive-demand structure that makes the preservation claim auditable, and far less crowded redesign advice.

Capacity honesty: at ~45–60 minutes of your review per audit, 100 audits is 75–100 hours. That is the real constraint and it is the point — phase 1 is deliberately unscalable because its output is discovery of the repeated work, not revenue. Cap it at 100 and stop.

The second incentive

"Contribute 3 assignments → receive the aggregate subject/grade assessment-pattern report." The report only ships when a subject/grade cell reaches N ≥ 20 (see §9 re-identification rule). Promise it honestly: "when we have enough submissions in your subject and grade band to say something true — we'll tell you when that is."

The flywheel, and its honest weak link

free audit → assignment corpus → aggregate patterns → shareable report → more submissions → sharper audit → paid department sprint. It works only if the aggregate patterns are genuinely useful to a teacher who didn't submit — otherwise there's nothing to share and the loop is just a lead magnet. Test that explicitly: does anyone forward the pattern report unprompted?

8 · Pass / fail thresholds — set before we start

Pre-registered so we can't rationalize afterwards. Measured on the Phase-2 cohort of 50.

PASS
Submission rate ≥ 30% of qualified teachers asked send an assignment within 7 days.
PASS
≥ 50% volunteer a written worry in their own words, unprompted, alongside the file.
PASS
≥ 60% reply after receiving the audit.
PASS
Objective-identification accuracy ≥ 75% — we correctly name what the teacher intended to measure in at least 3 of 4 cases, by their own confirmation.
PASS
≥ 40% report they used or adapted the redesign (behavioral, self-reported with specifics).
PASS
≥ 25% submit a second assignment; ≥ 15% reach three.
PASS
≥ 10% refer another teacher unprompted.
PASS
≥ 5 unprompted department/school inquiries across the 100 — or ≥ 3 introductions to a chair or administrator. This is the monetization gate and the most important one.
KILL
Submission rate < 15% · or objective accuracy < 60% · or zero department inquiries after 100 audits. Any one → stop and re-diagnose rather than scale.

Thresholds are judgment calls calibrated to be uncomfortable rather than flattering; the kill criteria matter more than the pass criteria.

9 · Ethics, privacy and copyright for building an assignment corpus

Hard rules

  • No student work, ever. Stated in the offer, repeated at upload. If student work arrives: delete, confirm deletion in writing, do not analyze.
  • No student PII, ever. No names, no IDs, no rosters, no grades.
  • The audit belongs to the teacher. We never share an individual audit with their department or administration without written permission. An audit saying "this may not measure what you intended" could be used against a teacher in evaluation — that risk is ours to eliminate, and saying so plainly is also our best trust signal.
  • Right of withdrawal. Any teacher can have their assignment and audit deleted on request, at any time, no reason required.
  • No model training on submissions without separate, explicit, opt-in consent — never bundled into the submission click.

Copyright routing (the real legal exposure)

Submitted assignments fall into three buckets and must be handled differently:

BucketHandling
Teacher-authoredFull use with consent: audit + corpus + aggregate patterns.
District- or department-mandatedAudit for the teacher; corpus inclusion as abstracted metadata only (task type, verbs, demand band) — never the verbatim text.
Publisher / textbook-derivedAudit for the teacher as fair-use analysis; excluded from corpus and aggregate outputs. Never reproduced or redistributed.

At submission the teacher confirms which bucket applies and that they have the right to share it. Aggregate reports contain patterns and de-identified paraphrase, never verbatim third-party assignments, and only publish a subject/grade cell at N ≥ 20 to prevent re-identification of a teacher or school.

11 · The strongest reason this whole idea may still fail

The redesigns add teacher work, and teachers have none to give.

Every verification mechanism that makes thinking visible — checkpoints, process evidence, oral defences, prediction steps — costs the teacher more time to run and more time to review. We already established that grading is 5–10 hrs/week, that teachers work ~54-hour weeks, and that genuinely personalized feedback at 140 students is arithmetically impossible. The 40% already asking for process evidence and the 14% doing oral defences are doing it for some assignments precisely because they cannot do it for all.

So the honest risk is this: the audit is right, the redesign is pedagogically correct, and the teacher still cannot run it. An assessment that is more valid but unaffordable in teacher-minutes will be admired and not adopted — and the free substitute (ban AI, blue books, lockdown browsers, as NYC just chose for 600,000 students) will keep winning on cost even though everyone agrees it is worse pedagogy and worse for multilingual and disabled students.

What this implies — and it is the through-line of everything we've built: a redesign is only adoptable if it ships with the machinery that makes the new verification cheap to run. Proof of Learning creates work; Teacher Time Back removes it. They are not competing products. The assessment audit is the wedge that gets us paid and gets us the corpus; the work-package agent is what makes the redesign survivable in a real classroom. If we sell the redesign without the second half, we will get admiration, a signed pilot, and quiet non-adoption by week six.

The cheapest way to test this specific risk in the first 100: for every teacher who says they used the redesign, ask one follow-up — "how long did it take you to run and review, compared to the original?" If the honest answer is consistently "longer, and I couldn't keep it up," we have found the real product requirement before building anything.
